\subsection{Illustrative Reconstructed Design Dialogue (Not a Transcript)}
\label{app:reconstructed}
\noindent\textbf{Label: AI-generated illustration.}
The dialogue below was written after implementation to explain the final
design through questions and answers. Both speakers' lines are reconstructed;
they are not quotations of exchanges that occurred and do not establish
student authorship of decisions or completed student verification. This
illustration supplements the recorded exchanges that follow. It does not
replace the assignment's required exact prompts and intermediate results.
Selected \emph{actual saved artifacts} are interleaved below and labeled
separately. Their placement explains the corresponding design topic; it does
not imply that the illustrative turns were their originating prompts. These
excerpts are not a claim that every intermediate output has been recovered.

\providecommand{\designturn}[2]{\par\medskip\noindent\textbf{#1 (illustrative):} #2\par}

\designturn{Student}{We need one practical problem solvable by network flow
and another that is NP-hard. Could volunteer staffing and emergency supply
depot selection give us clear models without making unrealistic claims?}

\designturn{AI}{Yes, with explicit simplifications. For staffing, represent
volunteers and shifts as a bipartite eligibility graph. For depot selection,
use a table listing which communities each candidate site can serve. The
report should explain that these models are motivated by public-service
planning, and that the experimental data are synthetic.}

\designturn{Student}{A volunteer might work several shifts. How do we stop
the algorithm from assigning someone to two simultaneous shifts?}

\designturn{AI}{The current model assumes shifts do not overlap. Eligibility
captures availability and qualifications; each volunteer also has a maximum
number of shifts. General overlap conflicts would need additional constraints
and a new correctness argument. Keeping that assumption explicit makes the
flow reduction valid for the stated task.}

\designturn{Student}{Under that assumption, what network should the
implementation construct, and how do we prevent one person from counting
twice toward the same shift?}

\designturn{AI}{Use source-to-volunteer arcs with workload capacities,
volunteer-to-shift arcs of capacity one for eligible pairs, and
shift-to-sink arcs with staffing-demand capacities. The unit middle arcs
prevent duplicate assignments to a shift. A complete schedule exists exactly
when the integral maximum flow equals total demand. The proof must map an
assignment to a flow and a flow back to an assignment.}

\designturn{Student}{Which maximum-flow algorithm gives us an implementation
that we can inspect and test? Would choosing a more advanced algorithm be
necessary for this project?}

\designturn{AI}{Edmonds--Karp is sufficient for these experiments. Its BFS
search and paired reverse residual arcs are straightforward to expose in
code. Reverse arcs matter: with eligible pairs $(w_1,j_1)$, $(w_1,j_2)$,
and $(w_2,j_1)$, an initial assignment of $w_1$ to $j_1$ must be revisable
to obtain both assignments. A targeted test should exercise that rerouting.}

\designturn{Student}{For the depot problem, is mapping its coverage table
to Set Cover enough to prove NP-hardness?}

\designturn{AI}{That direction alone is insufficient. To prove hardness,
start with an arbitrary Set Cover instance, create one community per element
and one candidate depot per set, and preserve the budget and membership
relation. Prove the yes-if-and-only-if correspondence. Then show that a
proposed depot selection is polynomially verifiable. The reverse mapping is
also included to address the assignment's reduction wording.}

\designturn{Student}{Does that proof also establish the result if coverage
means being within a fixed geographic distance?}

\designturn{AI}{No. The proof covers arbitrary explicit service relations.
It does not construct coordinates realizing every Set Cover instance. The
final model therefore uses a supplied coverage table and states that its
hardness claim does not establish every geometric variant.}

\designturn{Student}{What useful solution can we provide once exact depot
minimization is NP-hard? How can the report show its limitations?}

\designturn{AI}{Repeatedly choose the site covering the most uncovered
communities, breaking ties by input index. First check whether the union of
all coverage sets reaches every community. The greedy method then returns a
feasible cover, with an $H_n$ approximation guarantee. The six-community
example in the report makes its limitation concrete: greedy chooses three
sites, while two suffice.}

\designturn{Student}{For verification, can we compare against answers that
do not come from the same algorithm? And how should we evaluate runtime?}

\designturn{AI}{On small staffing instances, enumerate eligible-pair subsets
to establish the optimum independently of flow. On small depot instances,
enumerate site subsets in increasing size to establish the optimum. For
scaling, generate seeded sparse and dense families, exclude generation and
the exponential oracles from timing, and retain raw measurements. Count BFS
edge inspections and greedy membership tests as well as seconds.}

\designturn{Student}{If a timing slope is below a theoretical bound, should
we say the experiment disproves the analysis?}

\designturn{AI}{No. A worst-case upper bound need not be attained by random
inputs. Compare operation counts with their instance-specific bounds and
describe timing slopes as observations over the tested sizes. The singleton
coverage family gives an especially clear check: it forces exactly
$k(k+1)/2$ membership tests for this implementation.}
